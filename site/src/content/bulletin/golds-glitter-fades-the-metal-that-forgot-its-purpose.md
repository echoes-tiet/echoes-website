<p>
For decades, <strong>gold</strong> wore a single, unimpeachable crown: <em>the ultimate safe haven</em>.
Wars, inflation, collapsing currencies — gold always showed up. But something strange has happened in the
months since the American-Israeli strikes on Iran. The very shock that should have sent gold soaring
instead watched it tumble nearly <strong>15%</strong>, more than global equities. The hedge didn't hedge.
The insurance didn't pay out.
</p>

<p>
The explanation cuts deeper than a bad quarter. Gold has quietly transformed from a <em>disciplined store
of value</em> into something far less noble — a <strong>momentum trade</strong>, a meme asset dressed in
ancient credibility. Its <strong>60% surge</strong> between last summer and late February was turbocharged
not by geopolitical wisdom but by speculative flows into exchange-traded funds, whose holdings swelled by
<strong>25%</strong> to around <em>4,200 tonnes</em>. Central banks of Turkey, Poland and India were
simultaneously cashing profits to fund government spending. When the speculative bets unwound, gold fell
with the crowd, not against it. Bitcoin's shadow, once considered gold's pale digital imitation, now falls
uncomfortably close.
</p>

<p>
Yet to write gold's obituary would be premature. Its foundational case — protection against the
<em>debasement of money</em>, bloated sovereign debt, and governments tempted to inflate their way out —
remains structurally intact. The meme traders will eventually chase the next moon-bound asset, and when
they do, gold's long-term identity may quietly reassert itself. The question investors cannot yet answer
is a deceptively simple one: <strong>at what price does the last fair-weather believer finally walk
away?</strong>
</p>
