<p>
On <strong>9 April 2026</strong>, the <strong>United States</strong> and <strong>Iran</strong> reached a
high-stakes <em>“strategic truce”</em> in <strong>Islamabad</strong> after <strong>11 hours</strong> of
negotiations, marking a rare pause in direct hostilities following weeks of naval confrontations. Brokered
by <strong>Pakistan</strong>, the agreement focuses on <em>de-escalation in the Gulf</em>, with Iran
committing to restoring safe passage through the <strong>Strait of Hormuz</strong>, including the sharing
of maritime data on sea mine zones. The move comes after significant disruption to regional energy flows,
with nearly <strong>17%</strong> of <strong>Qatar’s LNG capacity</strong> reportedly affected. While the
agreement emphasizes de-escalation, Iran is understood to have sought constraints on external naval
presence in the Gulf, indicating <em>partial alignment</em> rather than full strategic convergence.
</p>

<p>
Yet, the accord’s limitations are as significant as its breakthroughs. It explicitly excludes the
<strong>Lebanon front</strong>, where <strong>Israel’s</strong> ongoing military operations continue
unabated. Within hours of the announcement, Israeli strikes in <strong>Beirut</strong> reportedly killed a
senior <strong>Hezbollah</strong> figure, underscoring how the conflict remains active beyond the truce’s
scope. Israel’s campaign against Hezbollah continues amid rising cross-border tensions, reinforcing
escalation beyond the truce’s scope. <strong>Tehran</strong> has accused <strong>Washington</strong> of
using the truce as a <em>“strategic shield”</em> for allied operations. At the same time, the U.S.
maintains that the Lebanon theatre involves <em>non-state actors</em> and falls outside its bilateral
engagement with Iran. This exclusion creates a <strong>fragmented security dynamic</strong>, where direct
US–Iran tensions are paused, but proxy conflict persists, leaving the broader region unstable.
</p>

<p>
Economically, the reopening of <strong>Hormuz</strong> offers <em>short-term relief</em> to global energy
markets and Gulf economies. Strategically, however, the agreement entrenches a <strong>fragmented regional
security order</strong>. By isolating US–Iran tensions from the broader Israel–Hezbollah conflict, the
truce sustains a pattern of <em>“controlled escalation”</em> rather than resolving underlying instability.
While <strong>Pakistan’s mediation</strong> provides a critical diplomatic off-ramp, the absence of a
wider regional ceasefire leaves the situation highly susceptible to <strong>rapid re-escalation</strong>.
</p>
