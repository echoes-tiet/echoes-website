<h2>The Moment</h2>
            <p>
              At <strong>8:25 PM on April 6, 2026</strong>, a reactor on the <strong>Tamil Nadu coastline</strong>
              quietly changed the course of India's energy future. The <strong>500 MWe Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor
                (PFBR)</strong> at <strong>Kalpakkam</strong> achieved <em>first criticality</em> — the point at which a
              nuclear chain reaction becomes fully self-sustaining — placing India in an exclusive club alongside just
              <strong>one other nation</strong> on the planet.
            </p>

            <h2>What Makes It Extraordinary</h2>
            <p>
              A <strong>fast breeder reactor</strong> does something almost counterintuitive: it produces more
              <em>fissile fuel</em> than it consumes. The PFBR's core, loaded with <strong>Uranium-Plutonium Mixed Oxide
                (MOX) fuel</strong>, is surrounded by a blanket of <strong>Uranium-238</strong>. Fast neutrons bombard
              this blanket and convert it into <strong>Plutonium-239</strong> — usable fuel for the next generation of
              reactors. In effect, the reactor <em>manufactures its own feedstock</em> while simultaneously generating
              electricity.
            </p>

            <h2>India's Three-Stage Plan</h2>
            <p>
              The achievement signals the start of <strong>Stage II</strong> of India's <strong>three-stage nuclear
                programme</strong> — a plan conceived by physicist <strong>Homi J. Bhabha</strong> in the 1950s. India
              holds limited uranium reserves, but sits on one of the world's largest <strong>thorium</strong> deposits
              along its coastline. The three-stage architecture was designed to eventually unlock that thorium wealth,
              using each stage's output to fuel the next, ending in a <em>self-sustaining thorium economy</em> that
              could power India for centuries.
            </p>

            <p>
              <strong>Stage I</strong> uses <strong>Heavy Water Reactors</strong> (already operational) to generate
              electricity and produce <strong>plutonium</strong> as a by-product.<br>
              <strong>Stage II</strong> — now live — uses that plutonium in <strong>fast breeder reactors</strong> to
              generate more fuel than is consumed.<br>
              <strong>Stage III</strong> will eventually use <strong>thorium-232</strong> converted into
              <strong>uranium-233</strong> to power an entirely <em>indigenous, uranium-independent fuel cycle</em>.
            </p>

            <h2>A Global First (Nearly)</h2>
            <p>
              With the PFBR achieving criticality, <strong>India</strong> becomes only the <strong>second country in the
                world</strong> after <strong>Russia</strong> to operate a commercial-scale fast breeder reactor. The
              reactor was designed entirely in India by the <strong>Indira Gandhi Centre for Atomic Research
                (IGCAR)</strong> and built by <strong>Bharatiya Nabhikiya Vidyut Nigam Ltd. (BHAVINI)</strong>, both
              under the <strong>Department of Atomic Energy</strong>. Over <strong>200 Indian industries</strong>,
              including <strong>MSMEs</strong>, contributed to its construction — a landmark for <em>Aatmanirbhar
                Bharat</em> in deep-tech manufacturing.
            </p>

            <h2>Two Decades in the Making</h2>
            <p>
              Construction began in <strong>2004</strong> with a target completion of <strong>2010</strong>. Technical
              challenges — most notably a problem with the <strong>fuel-handling machine</strong> operating inside
              <em>opaque liquid sodium coolant</em> where direct inspection is impossible — caused cascading delays.
              Engineers at <strong>IGCAR</strong> designed and fabricated an entirely new <strong>handling
                flask</strong> in under <strong>five months</strong>, eventually receiving <strong>AERB
                clearance</strong> to resume core loading in <strong>October 2025</strong>. The project cost rose from
              <strong>₹3,492 crore</strong> to over <strong>₹8,181 crore</strong> over its two-decade journey.
            </p>

            <h2>What Comes Next</h2>
            <p>
              <em>First criticality</em> is the starting gun, not the finish line. The PFBR will now undergo
              <strong>phased power ascension</strong>, <strong>performance testing</strong>, and <strong>safety
                validation</strong> before connecting to the <strong>national grid</strong> for commercial electricity
              generation. Success here paves the way for larger <strong>600 MWe fast breeder reactors</strong> and
              ultimately for <strong>Stage III thorium reactors</strong>. For a country with just <strong>8.78
                GW</strong> of nuclear capacity against a peak demand of <strong>242 GW</strong>, April 6 marks a
              turning point — not in kilowatts delivered today, but in the trajectory set for <strong>2047 and
                beyond</strong>.
            </p>
