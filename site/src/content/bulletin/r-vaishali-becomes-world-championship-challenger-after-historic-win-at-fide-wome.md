<h2>R. Vaishali Wins 2026 Women’s Candidates Tournament</h2>

            <p>Indian Grandmaster <strong><em>R. Vaishali</em></strong> has won the <strong><em>FIDE Women's Candidates
                  Tournament 2026</em></strong> in Cyprus, earning the right to challenge reigning Women’s World
              Champion <strong><em>Ju Wenjun</em></strong> for the title. The 24-year-old finished with <strong>8.5
                points out of 14</strong>, defeating <strong><em>Kateryna Lagno</em></strong> in a decisive final
              round—marking one of the most significant milestones in Indian chess in recent memory.</p>

            <p><strong> A Tournament of Resilience </strong></p>

            <p>The victory was far from straightforward. Vaishali began with <em>four draws</em> and then suffered a
              loss to top seed <strong><em>Zhu Jiner</em></strong>, leaving her outside the leading positions early on.
              What followed was a display of <strong>resilience</strong> that defined her tournament.</p>

            <p>A bounce-back win over Lagno looked set to be canceled out, but a <strong>one-move blunder</strong> by
              her opponent allowed Vaishali to build a streak of <strong>four wins in six games</strong>, putting her a
              full point clear of the field with three rounds to go.</p>

            <p><strong> Performance Under Pressure </strong></p>

            <p>She finished with <strong>5 wins, 7 draws, and only 2 losses</strong>—a record that reflects a player who
              absorbed pressure, stayed composed, and delivered when it mattered most. Notably, she entered as the
              <em>lowest-rated player</em> in the field, despite having previously crossed a <strong>2500+
                rating</strong> and securing back-to-back victories at the <strong><em>FIDE Women's Grand
                  Swiss</em></strong>.
            </p>

            <p><strong> A Journey Rooted in Chennai’s Chess Culture </strong></p>

            <p>Her rise has been methodical rather than sudden. A product of the same <em>Chennai chess culture</em>
              that produced <strong><em>Viswanathan Anand</em></strong>, Vaishali began playing at the age of five,
              earned her <strong>Woman Grandmaster</strong> title as a teenager, and has since entered the open
              <strong>Grandmaster</strong> ranks.
            </p>

            <p>Her performances over the past three years suggested that a breakthrough like this was not a matter of
              <em>if</em>, but <em>when</em>.
            </p>

            <p><strong> A Family Story Like No Other </strong></p>

            <p>The human dimension of her achievement is equally compelling. Vaishali described the victory as
              <strong>“a dream moment for our family”</strong> after fifteen years of dedication to chess. She was
              congratulated by her brother, Grandmaster <strong><em>R Praggnanandhaa</em></strong>, as she emerged from
              the playing hall.
            </p>

            <p>The siblings now stand among the most formidable chess players India has ever produced—a rare family
              story with few parallels in competitive sport. <strong><em>Viswanathan Anand</em></strong>, present at the
              venue, praised her <strong>“excellent preparation and resilience,”</strong> noting that she <em>“took the
                blows and delivered the punches.”</em></p>

            <p><strong> What Lies Ahead </strong></p>

            <p>With this victory, India now has the chance to hold both the <strong>overall</strong> and <strong>Women’s
                World Championship</strong> titles simultaneously. Vaishali will next face <strong><em>Ju
                  Wenjun</em></strong> in a championship match, with the <em>date and venue yet to be announced</em>.
            </p>

            <p>For a country that has come to expect excellence from its chess players, Vaishali’s achievement feels
              less like a surprise and more like a long-anticipated <strong>arrival</strong>.</p>
